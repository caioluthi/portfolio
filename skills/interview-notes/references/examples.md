# Failure modes to avoid

Each case shows the mistake, why it matters, and the fix. The examples use the fictional Second Avenue interviews.

## 1. An item with no quote behind it
**Bad:** Pain point: "Feels unsupported by senior leadership."
**Why it's wrong:** Nothing in the transcript says this. It is a plausible story that the notes invented, and a persona skill would later repeat it as fact.
**Fix:** Leave it out. If something in the transcript hints at it, record that moment as a signal with a neutral follow-up question.

## 2. A paraphrase presented as a quote
**Transcript:** "That's not something I can pull today."
**Bad:** > "We can't get that data."
**Why it's wrong:** The words changed, so the quote can no longer be checked against the source, and the verifier will reject it.
**Fix:** Use the exact words: > "That's not something I can pull today."

## 3. The interviewer's words used as evidence
**Transcript:** Interviewer: "So the delay is frustrating?" Interviewee: "Yeah."
**Bad:** Pain point: "Finds the reporting delay frustrating" (volunteered).
**Why it's wrong:** The interviewer supplied the idea and the interviewee agreed. That is weak evidence, and it is not volunteered.
**Fix:** If the item is worth keeping, tag it *prompted*, quote only the interviewee's words, and note that agreement alone is thin. Better still, raise a follow-up asking them to describe the delay in their own words.

## 4. A contradiction smoothed over
**Transcript:** Early on, "I never look at the weekly report." Later, "I check the weekly report every Monday."
**Bad:** "Reviews the weekly report occasionally."
**Why it's wrong:** The summary invented a middle position that the interviewee never took.
**Fix:** List both statements under "Tensions within this interview" with their quotes.

## 5. A solution filed as a pain point
**Bad:** Pain point: "Needs a dashboard."
**Why it's wrong:** A dashboard is their idea of a fix, not a problem they described.
**Fix:** File it under their view of the optimal solution. Add a separate pain point only if they described the problem behind it.

## 6. A frequency claim from one interview
**Bad:** "Data delays came up repeatedly and are a major theme."
**Why it's wrong:** One interview can't establish a theme. Counting across interviews is the job of cross-interview synthesis.
**Fix:** Describe the emphasis as the interviewee voiced it, for example that they called the delay "the biggest headache", with the quote.

## 7. An estimate promoted to a fact
**Transcript:** "I'd assume most of them did, if we'd been looking."
**Bad:** "Most underperforming contracts showed warning signs by the halfway point."
**Why it's wrong:** The interviewee said "assume". The claim also turned a guess into a finding.
**Fix:** Record it in the numbers table with basis *estimated (assumption)* and the exact quote, and tag any related item *speculation*.

## 8. Padding an empty section
**Bad:** Nice-to-haves: "Faster load times; a mobile app."
**Why it's wrong:** The interviewee never mentioned either. Filling the section to make the notes look complete is how invention starts.
**Fix:** Write `Not discussed`. Add a signal or a follow-up if the gap seems important.

## 9. A value inferred without evidence
**Bad:** Inferred values: "Values innovation; is tech-savvy."
**Why it's wrong:** Neither is tied to a quote, and "tech-savvy" is a trait, not a job-relevant value.
**Fix:** Keep only values you can anchor to a quote (for example, trust in the numbers, from a line about defending a figure nobody can reproduce), tagged *inferred*.
