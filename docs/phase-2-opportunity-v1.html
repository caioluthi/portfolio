Phase 2 · Opportunity & success metrics

Second Avenue finds out a Risk contract is underperforming only when it is too late to act.

Second Avenue is a fictional consignment retailer and every figure on this page is simulated. The evidence behind it is in the product discovery write-up.

TL;DR

Problem
Nobody sees a Risk contract falling behind its target until it has expired. About 15% of contracts underperformed over the last 12 months, costing roughly $3.6M a year in shortfall payouts.
Opportunity
Flagging contracts that pace below target while they are still running could recover about $110K to $1.4M a year, with a base case near $500K. Ops review time costs about $13K a year.
Success
Flag underperformers a median of at least 30 days before expiry, against under a week today, with no more than 40% false alarms and 25 new flags a week.
Pilot
Test a linear pacing flag, refreshed daily, against last year's contracts, and go live with Ops only if it meets the success targets.
Next
The PRD turns this into the requirements Engineering sizes.

The problem

Under a Risk contract, Second Avenue guarantees the consignor a target price. If sales fall short, the company pays the difference. If they beat the target, the company keeps a tiered share of the overage: 20% on the first $10K, 30% from $10K to $20K, and 70% above that, unless the contract negotiates other tiers.

That makes every Risk contract a standing exposure. The only way to manage it is to act while the contract is still running: move assets to a better channel, reprice them, or put them into a live auction.

Today nobody can see whether a contract is on pace to hit its target. Contract data sits in ConsignTrack, listings and sales sit in other systems, and the only exposure report is the quarterly Risk Book Summary, which looks backward. Ops relies on individual judgment and Finance sees the damage after the contract expires. A one-off view takes an analyst about 8 hours, arrives monthly at best, and drifts out of sync between systems.

What it costs

About 15% of contracts underperformed over the trailing 12 months, and 18% over the trailing 6. Estimated shortfall payouts come to roughly $3.6M a year. Nobody can say how many of those contracts showed warning signs at the halfway mark, or what could have been done if someone had seen them.

Reframe

Risk is a leading indicator. It should be measured as pacing against the target over the contract's lifetime, so a contract that is falling behind shows up while there is still time to intervene.

How might we give Ops and Finance a trusted, early view of which Risk contracts are pacing below target, so they can act before the contract expires?

Out of scope for this framing: predicting final outcomes with historical models, planned for a later version, and automating the interventions themselves.

The opportunity

Not every shortfall dollar can be saved. A dollar is recoverable only if the contract showed warning signs early enough, an action was still available, and that action worked. Each condition cuts the total, so the three shares multiply. Pick a scenario to see the funnel.

What this page does not claim

  • The $3.6M and the 15% and 18% rates come from a one-off manual pull, so they are estimates and not monitored figures.
  • Recovery when acted on is the weakest input. Once pacing is tracked, the product produces the data to replace it with a measured rate.
  • The sizing counts avoided shortfall only. It leaves out upside from better channel placement and the analyst time saved, about 8 hours per report today.

What success looks like

Reducing the $3.6M in shortfall payouts is the reason the product exists, but it is a poor primary metric. Whether a dollar is saved depends on the product noticing the contract, on Ops acting, and on the action working. The funnel above flagged that last step, the recovery rate, as its weakest assumption.

The product directly controls only the first step: how early it raises the flag. So detection lead time is the primary metric, and dollars saved sit above it as the outcome it should drive. If lead time improves and payouts don't fall, the problem is in the intervention, not the signal, and the metrics make that visible instead of blurring it.

The baseline lead time comes from the Ops interview: contracts are opened near the 90-day deadline, often with days rather than weeks left. Before launch, the backtest measures it properly by replaying today's practice, a manual review in the final week, over last year's contracts.

The primary metric and the false-positive guardrail pull against each other. A looser threshold flags contracts earlier, which raises lead time, and also flags more contracts that would have recovered on their own. The targets are set so that neither can be bought at the other's expense. How each metric is calculated is specified in the PRD.

The pilot bet

The pilot tests one claim: a simple pacing flag, computed daily from data Second Avenue already has, catches underperforming contracts weeks earlier than today without burying Ops in false alarms. If that holds, the dollars can follow. If it doesn't, nothing more sophisticated is worth building on top of it.

Why linear pacing first

Linear pacing compares sales to date with a straight line from zero to the target. It needs no history, Ops can check it in their head, and it is enough to test whether earlier flags are useful at all. Pacing against the historical sales curves of similar contracts is more accurate, especially for live auctions, and comes next once the pilot shows where linear pacing is wrong.

Why a daily refresh

Discovery left open how fresh the signal needs to be. David can work with weekly, Priya heard stakeholders ask for near-real-time, and she would trade speed for consistency. Tying cadence to the decision it supports settles it.

The decision is Ops choosing to reprice, switch channel or move a contract into an auction, and it plays out over days. A weekly refresh could spend up to 7 of the days the primary metric is trying to win. Real-time adds cost and makes it harder to keep the three source systems consistent, which is Priya's essential requirement. A daily refresh keeps every system on the same snapshot and gives Maria her morning list.

How we decide to go live

Before Ops relies on the flag, it is replayed over the last 12 months of closed contracts, whose final outcomes are already known. The pilot goes live with Ops when, on that backtest:

  • median detection lead time is at least 30 days,
  • at least 70% of underperformers are flagged by day 45,
  • no more than 40% of flags are false alarms,
  • new flags stay at or below 25 a week, and
  • every active contract reconciles across the three sources.

The 80% threshold and the day-15 floor are starting values, not commitments. The backtest sweeps both and picks the setting that best meets the lead-time target while staying inside the guardrails.

Risks to the bet

  • Back-loaded contracts look worse than they are. A contract whose assets are scheduled into a live auction late in the window will pace low until the auction and then catch up. Linear pacing will flag it. This is the main reason historical-curve pacing comes next, and the false-positive rate by channel will show how large the effect is.
  • Single-asset contracts are all or nothing. A contract covering one high-value item shows $0 realized until it sells, so pacing says little about it. False positives by contract size will show whether this segment needs separate handling.
  • The backtest can't prove the dollars. Payouts avoided depend on Ops acting on a flag, which history can't show. The pilot proves the signal; logging Ops actions, planned next, is what proves the dollars.
