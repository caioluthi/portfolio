Second Avenue 1 · Discovery 2 · Opportunity 3 · PRD 4 · Prototype

Phase 4 · Prototype & validation · In progress

Before asking Engineering to build it, test whether the flag works.

Second Avenue is a fictional consignment retailer and every figure on this page is simulated. The prototype is built against the PRD and judged by the go-live criteria set on the opportunity page.

TL;DR

Why
The whole product rests on one bet: a simple pacing flag catches failing contracts weeks earlier without burying Ops in false alarms. That is cheap to test and expensive to assume.
What
A timeboxed, throwaway prototype: the governed metrics on a simulated book of contracts, a replay of last year's contracts, Ops and Finance views, and plain-language questions on top.
Output
A go-live verdict, chosen flag settings, the failure cases sized, and a revised PRD.
So far
The simple flag failed: 80% of its flags were false alarms. Counting unsold inventory brought that to 36% on contracts it wasn't tuned on. The prototype also measured that 44% of underperformers can be caught with confidence by halfway, which resized the opportunity to about $320K a year. The morning list and Finance rollups are built, channel attribution is settled, and the Ops, Finance and Data feedback has been folded into the PRD.
Next
Phase 5 plans the production build from the revised PRD, with Engineering.

A prototype is worth building only if it answers questions that a document can't. These six decide whether the pilot goes ahead, how it is configured, and what changes in the requirements.

2. What it builds, and what it deliberately skips

The prototype covers the parts of the PRD that test the bet. It skips the parts that make a product dependable in production. Those are Engineering's to build, and listing them here is what keeps the prototype throwaway.

Builds
  • A simulated book of contracts, assets and sales, calibrated to the Phase 2 book, including late-selling auction contracts and single-asset contracts
  • Every calculated field in the PRD, under the same names
  • Lightweight versions of data product basics: every table and column documented, quality checks that fail the build, and one definition per metric
  • Flag history replayed day by day over last year's contracts, and the go-live report built on that replay
  • The ranked morning list and the Finance rollups
  • Plain-language questions that can read only the governed fields
Skips
  • Landing the three real source systems
  • Cross-source reconciliation
  • Reproducibility guarantees and scheduling
  • The weekly success scorecard
  • What makes it a production data product: a named owner, freshness and availability commitments, a catalog entry so it can be found, lineage, monitoring, access policies, and versioned data contracts with the source systems

5. Results

Q1 · Does the bet hold? Not as first written.

The first flag compared sales to date with a straight line to the target. Replayed over the 1,968 contracts that closed in the last 12 months, it caught underperformers early: 80% of them, a median of 75 days before expiry, against 33% in the final week today. But 80% of its flags were false alarms, double the limit, and none of the 40 combinations of settings tested got below 70%.

Accuracy depended almost entirely on timing:

Days into the contract15–3030–4545–6060–7575–90
Share of flags that were right19%26%38%56%90%

With 15% of contracts underperforming, a flag in the first month was barely better than picking contracts at random. Early in a contract, being behind mostly means the big items haven't sold yet, not that they won't.

A better signal, tested once on contracts it wasn't tuned on

Two changes were tested together, with the design, grids and selection rule committed before the run. Settings were chosen on contracts that closed 6 to 12 months ago and scored once on the last 6 months.

  • B · Count what is still unsold. Project the final value from sales so far plus the appraised value of unsold items, priced by how the contract's sold items have fetched against their appraisals.
  • C · Add watch and act tiers. Only flag act once enough of the contract has sold to trust the signal; otherwise watch.

A is the linear flag from step 2, scored the same way. Today is the final-week review.

Counting unsold inventory fixed the false alarms, from 66% to 37%, and halved the weekly flags. The tiers added a little on top: 6 more points of recall and a watch list that rarely tops 10 contracts.

Q2 · What should the settings be?

Chosen by the rule fixed before the run, using contracts that closed 6 to 12 months ago: flag when the projection falls below 95% of target, from day 21, with a cushion worth 15% of target so that one or two cheap early sales can't swing the projection, and move to act once 15% of appraised value has sold.

Q3 · Where does the flag get it wrong?

The suspects were contracts whose items sell late at auction, and single-item contracts. Auction-heavy contracts were the worst, with 87% of their flags false, but they are only 4% of the book, and every channel and contract size sat between 77% and 83%. No segment explained the failure, so excluding one wouldn't fix it; the flag itself had to change.

The new flag fixed the false alarms but falls short on catching underperformers by halfway. Staying under the 40% false-alarm limit, the best any setting could do was about 41% by day 45; catching 70% meant going back to about 70% false alarms. About half of the contracts that will end short still look healthy at day 45 on sales and appraisals alone. The 70% target came from a judgment in the Phase 2 sizing, so the prototype replaced it with what it measured, and the target moved with it, as recorded below.

Q4 · How should a contract count in rollups by channel?

By its items, not as a whole. Channel and asset type belong to items, and most flagged contracts mix them: 21 of the 37 contracts flagged act had no channel holding 80% of their unsold value. Assigning each contract wholly to its main channel moved 7 to 15% of exposure between channels and understated live auction by 13%. So each contract's projected shortfall is split over its unsold items by appraised value, because what is still unsold is where the remaining risk sits and what Ops can still reprice or move. Every rollup then adds up to the book total, which the build now checks on every run.

6. What Ops and Finance said

The three people from discovery each saw the prototype's output for its "today", 30 September, and got the same opening question: what would you do with this tomorrow morning? Like the discovery interviews, the sessions are simulated. The numbers they react to are the prototype's.

Maria, Contract Operations Manager

Shown: the morning list as built in step 3, act and watch tiers.

"Number one has nothing left to sell. That's a Finance conversation, not mine."

Session notes

What would you do with this list tomorrow morning?

"Start at the top, except I'd skip number one. It has nothing left to sell. That's a Finance conversation, not mine. If it's on my list I'll look at it every morning and feel bad about it, and it pushes the ones I can still fix down the page."

Take the next one: seven days left. What can you do?

"Price. That's it. Live auction catalogs close two weeks before the sale, so anything with under fourteen days can't go into an auction anymore. I'd want the list to tell me that, so I'm not checking the calendar for every contract."

Thirty-odd contracts flagged act. Is that workable?

"If they don't all change every morning, yes. What I really need is which ones are new since yesterday and which ones I've already worked. Otherwise I'm rereading the same list."

What's missing?

"Tell me which items are in the wrong place. If a painting is sitting on the app, I know it belongs in an auction, but I have to open the contract and go find it. I don't need the system to tell me what to do. I need it to show me where to look."

And the contracts on watch?

"Three is fine. I'd glance at them on Fridays. If watch got to thirty, I'd stop reading it."

  • Contracts with nothing left to sell don't belong on Ops' list. They are 4 of the 37 act contracts and $78K of exposure, already lost.
  • What Ops can still do depends on time. Of the 33 act contracts that still have something to sell, 12 have under 14 days left, so repricing is their only lever.
  • She wants facts about where things are, not recommendations. That matches what she said in discovery: "I know my options. I just can't see the problem in time." 25 of the 33 have unsold items on a channel where their type sells at least 15 points below its best channel, $359K of appraised value.
  • "Already worked" needs a record of Ops actions, the same data that would measure the recovery rate. That item in Next scope now has two reasons behind it.

David, Finance Manager

Shown: exposure by region, channel and asset type, and 13 weeks of daily exposure.

"If I report that number up, someone will ask what happened every week, and the answer will mostly be nothing."

Session notes

What would you do with this tomorrow?

"Send the region view to the regional directors. I'd have guessed the Midwest, but I've never been able to prove it. Half the exposure from about a quarter of the book is a conversation I can now have."

Could you report exposure at risk upward?

"The trend, yes, but not the daily number. It moved between $229K and $366K over the summer. If I report that up, someone will ask what happened every week, and the answer will mostly be nothing. Give me a four-week average next to it."

What would you do with the $336K itself?

"My instinct is to book it as the shortfall reserve for the quarter. It's better than what I use now."

Which part of it are you surest about?

"The money on contracts with nothing left to sell. That's not a projection anymore, it's a bill. Show me that on its own."

And watch?

"Keep it separate. Thirty thousand on watch is a footnote, not a number I'd report."

  • The surest number is the locked-in part, $78K of the $336K today. It's the same change Maria asked for, seen from the other side.
  • The daily figure is too noisy to report. A 4-week average goes beside it: $307K today, between $271K and $336K since July.
  • Exposure at risk is not a reserve. It is built to rank and flag contracts, and it has never been tested as a forecast of payouts. Using it for accounting would need its own calibration against actual payouts. The PRD now says so, so the number isn't used for something it can't support.

Priya, Data Analyst

Shown: the data contract in the PRD and the checks that run on every build.

"'Appraised value at intake' doesn't exist as a clean field today."

Session notes

Looking at the data contract, what worries you?

"The appraisal field. In ConsignTrack, when Ops reprices an item, the estimate gets overwritten with the new price. And some lots get appraised as one line, not item by item. So 'appraised value at intake' doesn't exist as a clean field today."

What would you need?

"Keep the intake appraisal as its own field nobody can edit, next to the current asking price. And tell me every day how many items are missing one, because the flag will quietly get worse if that number drifts."

How would you handle the lots?

"Split the appraisal across the items using the appraiser's notes, or treat the lot as one item. Either works, as long as it's the same every time."

Anything you'd keep from the prototype?

"The checks that fail the build. If the rollups don't add up, nothing ships. I never had that. I found my mistakes when David did."

  • The flag's key input isn't clean in the source system. The intake appraisal becomes its own field that repricing can't overwrite. Until now that was only an open question.
  • Completeness becomes a requirement. Appraisal coverage is reported on every refresh, and contracts below 95% are marked instead of silently projected. The prototype now checks it on every build; the simulated book is complete.
