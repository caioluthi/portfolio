1. What success looks like

Reducing the $3.6M in shortfall payouts is the reason the product exists, but it is a poor primary metric. Whether a dollar is saved depends on the product noticing the contract, on Ops acting, and on the action working. Phase 2 flagged that last step, the recovery rate, as its weakest assumption.

The product directly controls only the first step: how early it raises the flag. So detection lead time is the primary metric, and dollars saved sit above it as the outcome it should drive. If lead time improves and payouts don't fall, the problem is in the intervention, not the signal, and the metrics make that visible instead of blurring it.

The baseline lead time comes from the Ops interview: contracts are opened near the 90-day deadline, often with days rather than weeks left. Phase 5 will measure it properly by replaying a "final-week manual review" policy on the synthetic data.

The primary metric and the false-positive guardrail pull against each other. A looser threshold flags contracts earlier, which raises lead time, and also flags more contracts that would have recovered on their own. The targets above are set so that neither can be bought at the other's expense.

A worked example

A $50,000 contract on a 90-day window has sold $11,000 by day 30. Linear pacing expects a third of the target by then, about $16,700, so pacing is 66% and the contract is flagged. Projected linearly, it finishes near $33,000, a projected shortfall of about $17,000.

On this contract's actual sales path, the flag first fires on day 15, the first day it is allowed to. Today it would be noticed around day 85. That difference, 75 days of lead time against about 5, is what the primary metric measures.

3. Refresh cadence: daily

Discovery left open how fresh the signal needs to be. David can work with weekly, Priya heard stakeholders ask for near-real-time, and she would trade speed for consistency. Tying cadence to the decision it supports settles it.

The decision is Ops choosing to reprice, switch channel or move a contract into an auction, and it plays out over days. A weekly refresh could spend up to 7 of the days the primary metric is trying to win. Real-time adds cost and makes it harder to keep the three source systems consistent, which is Priya's essential requirement. A daily batch refresh, landing before Ops starts the day, keeps every system on the same snapshot and gives Maria the ranked morning list she described.

4. How v1 will be judged

This is a lab build, so success is judged by a backtest. The Phase 5 synthetic data generator produces contracts whose final outcomes are known, including a realistic share that pace below target. Replaying the daily flag over that history produces every metric above.

v1 passes when, on the backtest:

  • median detection lead time is at least 30 days,
  • at least 70% of underperformers are flagged by day 45,
  • no more than 40% of flags are false alarms,
  • new flags stay at or below 25 a week, and
  • every active contract reconciles across the three sources.

The 80% threshold and the day-15 floor are starting values, not commitments. The backtest sweeps both and picks the setting that best meets the lead-time target while staying inside the guardrails.

5. Scope

v1 · building now
  • Linear pacing and the v1 risk flag
  • Projected shortfall and exposure at risk
  • Daily batch refresh with cross-source reconciliation
  • Ranked daily list of flagged contracts for Ops
  • Book rollups by region, channel, asset type and consignor for Finance
  • Natural-language questions over these metrics
v2 · stated next
  • Pacing against the historical sales curve of similar past contracts, by asset type, channel and region
  • Suggested interventions per flagged contract
  • Logging of Ops actions, so the recovery rate can be measured instead of assumed
Not planned
  • Automated repricing or channel moves
  • Real-time streaming
  • What-if simulation of moving assets between channels
  • Optimizing the upside tiers

6. Known limitations of v1

  • Back-loaded contracts look worse than they are. A contract whose assets are scheduled into a live auction late in the window will pace low until the auction and then catch up. Linear pacing will flag it. This is the main reason historical-curve pacing is v2, and the backtest will report the false-positive rate by channel to show how large the effect is.
  • Single-asset contracts are all or nothing. A contract covering one high-value item shows $0 realized until it sells, so pacing says little about it. The backtest will report false positives by contract size so this segment can be handled separately if it dominates the noise.
  • The outcome metric can't be fully proven in a lab. Payouts avoided depend on Ops acting, which the synthetic data can only assume. v1 proves the signal; v2's action logging is what would prove the dollars.
