Phase 6 · Package & deliver

Built with AI. Decided by me.

This case study was built with Claude as a working partner, from interview synthesis to the prototype's code. This page is the honest account: what AI did, what I decided, where I overrode it, and how its work was checked.

TL;DR

AI did
Drafting, synthesis, the sizing arithmetic, the prototype's SQL and Python, and the backlog's first cut.
I did
Chose the problem, set the framing and the bar for success, made every scope and sequencing call, and decided what the evidence meant.
Checked by
Tests that AI couldn't grade itself on: a backtest on contracts the flag wasn't tuned on, and a 30-question evaluation scored against SQL.
Reusable
The repeatable steps are packaged as Claude skills in the repository, so the workflow runs the same way next time.

1. Who did what

AI is fast at producing a first version. The product calls are where the work is, so those stayed with me in every phase.

PhaseWhat AI didWhat I decided
01 · DiscoveryDrafted personas and interview transcripts from my notes on the real problem; structured jobs to be done.The use case, the three vantage points worth interviewing, and that risk means pacing over the contract's life, not a miss at expiry.
02 · OpportunityBuilt the sizing funnel and ran the low, base and high cases.The assumptions behind each step, the primary metric, the guardrails, and what the pilot must prove to go live.
03 · PRDDrafted the requirements and field lists in a fixed structure.What is in and out of scope, and that the PRD says what, not how: the why stays on the opportunity page, the how and when with Engineering.
04 · PrototypeWrote the data generator, the SQL views, the backtest and the question layer.What the prototype had to answer, the go-live bar it was judged against, and what to do when the first flag failed.
05 · BacklogBroke the PRD into epics and stories and synced them to GitHub Issues.How many epics, what each one must deliver, the release sequence, and that estimates belong to Engineering.

2. Where I overrode it

The moments where the first answer wasn't the right one, and what I did instead.

  1. Phase 01Replaced an invented use case with one I lived.The first idea was a fictional working-capital problem. I switched to Risk contracts, a problem I worked on professionally, so every assumption could be tested against real experience.
  2. Phase 02Caught numbers that didn't reconcile.An average contract of about $8K couldn't produce about $4M a year in shortfalls at the stated miss rate. I raised the average to about $50K, where a contract can hold one high-value item or a lot.
  3. Phase 04Kept Claude away from free-form SQL.The question layer can only call fixed, governed queries, or decline. A prompt asking it to use the agreed definitions isn't enough; the tools make it impossible to do otherwise.
  4. Phase 05Cut eleven epics to six.The first breakdown mirrored the requirements one to one. I regrouped it into outcomes someone can sign off on, with two or three stories each, and left the technical breakdown to refinement with the Tech Lead.
  5. Phase 05Refused to wait ten weeks for value.The first roadmap had one launch at week 10. I asked what could ship without the flag, and the contract book became a week-4 release that ends the manual report on its own.

3. How AI's work was checked

Polished output proves nothing. Each piece that mattered had to pass a test it couldn't talk its way through.

WhatThe checkResult
The risk flagReplayed last year's contracts, with settings chosen on one set and judged on contracts the flag hadn't seen.The first design failed: 80% false alarms over last year, 66% on the held-back contracts. The revised flag reached 36% on those same contracts, inside the 40% guardrail.
Plain-language answers30 questions, including six to decline and four ambiguous ones, scored against SQL on the same governed views. Three runs per model; a question passes only if it passes every run.Sonnet 30 of 30 in every run. Haiku 29 of 30, at a twentieth of the cost.
The sizingEvery step of the funnel traced to a stated assumption, then the first step replaced with the prototype's measured value.Base case revised down, from about $500K to about $320K a year.
The backlogA script checks that every story traces to a requirement before anything syncs to GitHub.Fifteen stories, each tied to a DPR.

4. The skills

When a step worked, I packaged it as a Claude skill: instructions, rules for the judgment calls, and scripts that check the output. The next project starts from a workflow, not a blank prompt.

Phase 01

interview-notes

Turns one interview transcript into structured notes where every item is backed by a verbatim quote, and what was said stays separate from what is read into it. A script checks every quote against the transcript.

View the skill ↗

Phase 03

opportunity-to-prd

Turns an opportunity brief into a PRD in the same seven sections every time, with every success metric broken down into fields Engineering can build, and revises it when new evidence arrives without renumbering anything. A script checks that every metric's chain of fields closes.

View the skill ↗

Phase 05

prd-to-backlog

Turns a PRD into outcome epics and functional stories, records what Engineering decides in refinement, and syncs the result to GitHub Issues.
